# 0014. The brand book makes no claim about how an image was made

Date: 2026-08-22
Status: accepted

## Context

The v2 book made provenance a principle. A brand principle read "Real people, real campuses"; the
imagery chapter opened with "Real people on a real campus, photographed as they are"; six further
pages carried some form of "no generated faces, no generated screens, ever", "no face is ever
generated to avoid a release", "never a generated image".

A brand book cannot keep that promise, and it is the wrong promise to make. It is a claim about how
every image in every future campaign was produced, enforced by nobody, verifiable by no reader, and
already false of the book itself — the photography in it is stock, licensed under the Unsplash
License and labelled as direction examples rather than brand assets. A rule the book cannot audit
is decoration.

What the imagery chapter is actually for is **direction and consistency** — what a photograph looks
like, not where it came from.

## Decision

**The book states no rule, prohibition or claim about how an image was produced.** Every mention of
generated content leaves the book.

The imagery chapter's axis changes from *"it is real"* to *"it looks like this, not like that"*.
The rules on light, colour, crop and treatment are unchanged and were always about appearance.

**What stays:** FERPA, and the institution's own release process, for the case of real photography
made on a client campus. That is a legal obligation to a named third party, not a claim about
production.

**What does not enter:** licensing, tooling, or image clearance. The book does not treat them.

## Consequences

- The brand principle "Real people, real campuses" is replaced by one about consistency of
  direction, which the book can hold itself to.
- The imagery chapter goes from seven pages to three, because the provenance material was most of
  what filled the extra four.
- This decision is about the *book*. It is not a licence to generate imagery, and it does not
  change what the team chooses to commission; it changes what the brand publishes as a rule.
